Feedback arrives as a support ticket on Tuesday, a Slack line from a VP on
Thursday, and five people in a usability session next week. Six months later
nobody can remember who said what, the early notes are gone, and the summary
that survived into the roadmap deck cannot be traced to a single human being.

Strata files each piece as its own dated layer and refuses to let the layers
blur.

Its spine is three sections that most feedback notes collapse into one:
**Said** carries verbatim only, **Meant** carries your reading and is marked as
yours, and **Decided** carries what was actually done — usually nothing, and
that emptiness is the point. It is what makes the `open` report possible: the
things a team keeps hearing and keeps not answering.

The second rule is an evidence bar, and it is the reason this exists rather
than a folder of notes. One participant is a quote. Two is a possible pattern,
and you say the number out loud. Three independent participants is a pattern.
Five people in one room who heard each other are one signal, not five — and a
stakeholder's opinion is never pooled with user evidence.

Identities never enter the record. Participants become `P-01`, stakeholders
`S-01`, and the mapping lives in a file the skill gitignores before it writes
anything else. Reports carry pseudonyms too, including internal ones.

**Watch for**: it will not give you a tidy theme from thin data. Asked for
patterns across a fortnight of entries, it will tell you the record is young
and name the counts instead. That is the intended behavior and it is initially
annoying.

**Built and tested against a baseline.** Run without it, an agent filed real
names and emails into committable files, stamped `severity: high` onto its own
guesses, and called two people "a convergent theme." With it, all four
corrected.
