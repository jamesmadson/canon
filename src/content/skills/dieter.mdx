Ask an agent to review an overdesigned component and it does something
strange: it writes a genuinely competent critique — and then buries it. In
the baseline this skill was built against, the reviewer produced twenty-plus
findings across five frameworks, graded them with traffic-light emoji, mixed
additions in with removals, and shipped an entire rewritten component nobody
asked for. Thorough, and useless as a decision aid.

Dieter is the counter-shape. A review is five parts and nothing else: the
surface's **job** in one sentence, **removals** each priced as a cost to that
job, at least one thing **kept** with its reason, at most one **missing**
piece of function, and the **smallest next step**. Frequency is the
multiplier throughout — a flourish on a daily surface costs its price every
day.

The rules with teeth:

- **Findings, not files.** It will not write a "revised version" unless you
  ask after reading the review. An unrequested rewrite makes the decision
  for you and hides the reasoning inside diff noise.
- **A review that keeps nothing has not understood the design** — it has
  only disliked it. The Kept section is mandatory.
- **Taste needs a mechanism.** "Gradients feel dated" is not a finding;
  "the gradient band pushes the renewal date below the fold on a card
  checked daily" is.
- **No severity theater.** No icons, no scores — ranking is prose order,
  and the reason is the finding.

Where a project keeps a Canon record, Dieter checks removals against it
first — a decision the team already made and recorded outranks the skill's
defaults, cited by entry.

The name is a tribute, and the phrase is rendered correctly — *less, but
better*, not "less is more," which belongs to Mies. Verified against its
baseline: same overdesigned billing card, and the skill produced eight
priced removals, one kept, one missing, zero files written.
