## Said

*LinkedIn post by P-18, a marketing podcast host, posted around
2026-08-18; James supplied the post. P-18 is pseudonymized per the
LinkedIn precedent. The method's originator, Carl Alviani, is named as
published work: he described Narrative Prototyping publicly on the
LiftKit Marketing Podcast, and the technique is attributed to him and his
agency team.*

> "What if you prototyped a product the same way you'd write a story?"

The structure, as reported: Aristotle's five parts of drama pointed at an
unglamorous product problem (the example given is airline digital
ticketing across app and kiosk), with the user as the hero.

> "You start with their steady state, the ordinary way things work now.
> Then the inciting incident, the thing that pulls them toward your
> product. Then the struggles as they learn it. Then the payoff. And
> finally the new normal, how their life is different than before they
> started."

On why it works:

> "Writing is cheap and fast. You can draft the whole experience, hand
> it to your team, and watch them immediately start arguing about what's
> wrong with it. Change two sentences and it's updated. You've
> pressure-tested the product before anyone built a thing."

> "Most teams design first and find the story later."

## Meant

Three readings, all unverified beyond the post.

First, and most useful: the stated reason the method works is that prose
is *universally reviewable*. Everyone on a team can read a paragraph and
argue with it; no one needs a repo, a Figma seat, or a build. That is the
same property coded prototypes lose, approached from the opposite
direction. Interpretation, not testimony: P-18 is describing a method's
advantages, not reporting a lost review surface, so this does **not**
pool with the four voices on the review-surface pattern. It is a
mechanism note, not a fifth voice.

Second: "change two sentences and it's updated" is the cheap-iteration
argument that Canon makes about branches. A narrative and a branch family
are both cheap-to-fork representations of an experience. The five-part
structure could order a prototype family the way a journey map does
(D-32's journey arrangement mode), which is a real design idea if Survey
is ever built.

Third: a skill candidate. The five parts are a checkable shape, which is
the property the skill lineup requires. Not proposed, not sequenced;
noted so it is not lost. Any such skill would credit Alviani's method by
name rather than absorbing it, per the references-not-copies rule.
